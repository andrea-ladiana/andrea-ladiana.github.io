A memory bigger than its brain

A network that stores pairs, and whose capacity grows exponentially with its size. It remembers almost anything it is told. What it cannot do is invent an answer it was never told, unless the data make guessing easy.

20neurons, in two layers of 10
11,522pairs stored, each returned with 99% of its neurons right

01

Ask with one, answer with the other

This memory stores pairs. Show it one half and it returns the other. Here it holds 26 pairs, each capital letter with its small letter, in two layers of 400 neurons. Click a letter to ask, and blur the question.

stored pairs · bar: share of the vote · click to ask
0.70
vote for the right pair – answer – letters answered right, 2 blurred questions each –

Every stored pair votes on the answer. Its weight is eN(m1m2 − 1), where m1 and m2 say how well each layer matches it. Because N = 400 sits in the exponent, a slightly better match outweighs all the others combined. The first guess is a blur: with only the question known, nobody can be weighed yet. From the next sweep on, the exponential picks one pair. It usually picks the right one; F, L and M are the exceptions, because that blur already leans toward another small letter. The classic linear vote gives every pair a say proportional to its match. Letters look alike, so the answer melts into a smudge.

02

Exponentially many

Store P random pairs in two layers of N neurons, start from one of them and let the network update. If it stays put, the pair is a memory. Add pairs until it slips.

–
reduced load γ – theory, one sweep – paper, five sweeps – your run –

Each extra pair adds noise of order e−Nρ, with ρ2 ≈ 1.35, so the capacity grows as eNρ. One more neuron per layer multiplies it by about 3.8. At N = 10 there are only 1,024 different patterns per layer, yet more than ten thousand pairs stay put: a memory here is the pair, one point among 220. A classic Hopfield network holds about 0.14 memories per neuron.

More layers, more room

Bind L layers instead of two and the rate ρL grows. It comes from a single equation, tanh x = x/[2(L − 1)], solved here as you move the slider.

3
saddle x* – rate ρL – capacity at N = 10, e10ρ –

The rate approaches L log 2, the logarithm of the number of joint states per neuron: the network can file an exponential share of all its configurations. The bill grows at the same rate. Each sweep reads every stored pair, so running near capacity at N = 64 would mean reading about 1037 pairs per step. The capacity describes which states are fixed points, not a regime any machine can reach.

03

How wrong can the question be?

Three layers of 30 neurons hold 35,224 random triples. Start from a damaged copy of one, each neuron kept with probability (1 + r)/2, and apply one sweep. Does it come home?

0.50
neurons flipped – exponent ε3, annealed · typical – your run –

Two estimates bracket the answer. The typical one follows the most likely damage and is pessimistic. The annealed one averages over all damage patterns, and the rare lucky ones dominate that average. The simulations sit on the annealed curve. On the right, the price: Pc(r) ~ eNεL(r) stays exponential down to the threshold where ε hits zero. Wider networks start higher but cross zero sooner: they store more and forgive less.

04

One question, one answer

Many questions may share an answer. One question with several answers is a different matter. File the question “?” under q different digits and ask it.

3
first sweep, overlap with each answer – independent answers, theory – settles on –

All q pairs match the question equally well, so they tie in the vote. The first sweep returns their pixel-by-pixel majority. For independent answers its overlap with each one is about √(2/πq): the curve, checked by the dots. The digits sit higher because they share most of their pixels. Let it settle and it locks onto one of them, chosen by details of the blend, not by anything stored. Either way the question was never stored. That is why the real data below keep each cue with a single target. Many cues per target is fine: it is what real data look like.

05

Two chains name the antigen

A T cell recognises its target, a short peptide called the epitope, through two receptor chains, α and β. VDJdb lists receptor–epitope pairs confirmed in the lab. The paper keeps 1,052 of them, one epitope per receptor. Here 789 are stored, as three layers of 128 neurons.

epitope recalled – true epitope – your tally –

From both chains a stored receptor is recalled essentially without error. From one chain alone, only about four times in five: each chain carries information the other lacks. For a receptor it has never seen, the network names the right epitope about one time in ten. That is twice a shuffled-label control and twenty times chance, but far below its memory.

06

Same network, different words

Now the pairs are sentences and their intents, from CLINC150: 16,868 requests stored over 150 intents, in two layers of 128 neurons. Type a request in English.

Loading 16,868 sentences…

all

Storage rule, dynamics and closed forms are the same as for receptors. Yet a new sentence finds its intent 0.58 of the time, against 0.11 for a new receptor. The encoder makes the difference: paraphrases share words, so their codes land close together. Swap it for a text-blind random code and memory survives while new sentences fall to chance (0.008). The memory belongs to the network. Generalisation belongs to the encoder.
